About

This course teaches AI literacy through infrastructure. Rather than treating AI as an abstract or inevitable technology, students learn to see it as a system built on physical materials, economic relations, governance decisions, and social choices — all of which can be studied, questioned, and contested.

Moving through four thematic layers — from mines and data centers to platform ecosystems, policy regimes, and the people AI most affects — students develop the conceptual vocabulary and critical tools to analyze AI wherever they encounter it. No coding required. Some discomfort expected.

Thematic Architecture

layer 01
Material Infrastructure

Mines, data centers, undersea cables, energy, supply chains, planetary costs.

layer 02
Data & Platform Ecosystems

Classification systems, training data, linguistic bias, platform power, algorithmic displacement.

layer 03
Governance & Regulation

AI policy, open vs. proprietary models, digital sovereignty, labor law, archival control.

layer 04
Social / User Infrastructure

Borders, bodies, surveillance, adversarial design, tech labor, Global South users.

Course Outcomes

  • Recognize AI as infrastructure — a system with physical, economic, and political dimensions — rather than a neutral or magical technology.
  • Apply a four-layer framework (material / data / governance / social) to analyze any AI system critically.
  • Trace the supply chains, labor conditions, and governance structures that underlie AI products students encounter every day.
  • Evaluate claims made about AI — in the news, by companies, in policy — using primary sources and scholarly frameworks.
  • Produce original analysis of an AI system, dataset, or platform using methods learned in the course.
  • Situate AI within histories of colonialism, labor, and infrastructure, and identify who benefits and who bears the costs.

Working Agreement

No technical background required. This is a critical studies course. What matters is careful reading, honest thinking, and willingness to question things that seem obvious.

On using AI tools. You may use AI tools in this course. But the course is about AI — so every time you use one, you are using the object of study. You will be asked to reflect on those uses explicitly.

Primary sources over summaries. Read the actual texts. They are stranger, more specific, and more useful than any summary.

Disagreement is productive. The readings do not always agree with each other. Neither do the scholars cited. Neither will you. That tension is the work.

🧸 Show & Tell

Your Goal

Each week, 1–2 students share a 5–8 minute presentation at the start of class. Bring something that helps us think about AI as infrastructure — a news story, a dataset, a photograph, a policy document, a visualization, a product, a zine. It does not have to be academic. It has to be specific and connected to what we're reading.

Instructions

  • Show us a specific artifact — not a topic in general, but a particular thing.
  • Explain what it is, who made it, and who it affects.
  • Connect it to the week's thematic layer.
  • End with one question for the class.

Examples to pick from (or bring your own)

Infrastructure objects: an undersea cable map, a data center's water usage disclosure, a coltan mine photograph, a GPU shortage news story, an AWS outage post-mortem

Policy artifacts: EU AI Act provisions, Peru's Free Software Bill, the UVA Archival AI Protocol, an EEOC guidance on AI hiring tools

Practitioner work: Forensic Architecture, Stop LAPD Spying Coalition's Algorithmic Ecology, Estampa's Cartography of Generative AI, the Bits in the Machine zine, Rest of World reporting

AI products: a content moderation decision you disagree with, an AI-generated image and its prompt, an LLM response on a sensitive topic in two different languages

Assignments

Assignment 1
"Infrastructure Autopsy"
due: week 2

Your Goal

Choose one AI system you actually use or encounter — a search engine, a feed algorithm, a chatbot, a hiring tool, a content filter — and dissect it. Not what it does on the surface, but what it runs on. You are not analyzing the interface. You are analyzing the infrastructure.

Instructions

  • Choose one AI system and research each of the four layers:
    • Material: What hardware does it run on? Where? What energy does it use?
    • Data: What was it trained on? By whom? Under what conditions?
    • Governance: Who owns it? What terms govern its use? Who was excluded from those decisions?
    • Social: Who uses it? Who is harmed by it? What labor sustains it?
  • Create a hand-drawn or digital infrastructure map showing how the four layers connect.
  • Write a 300–400 word autopsy: what did you find? What surprised you? What couldn't you find out, and why?

Deliverable

Infrastructure map + written autopsy. Submit as PDF.

Grading

Depth of research across all four layers40%
Clarity and insight of the infrastructure map30%
Quality of written autopsy30%
Assignment 2
"Follow the Data"
due: week 3

Your Goal

Pick one dataset that was used to train a real AI system and trace where it came from. Data doesn't appear from nowhere — it is collected, curated, annotated, and cleaned by people, usually under conditions that are invisible to the end user. Your job is to make those conditions visible.

Choose One

  • A large public training dataset (e.g. Common Crawl, LAION-5B, ImageNet, C4, BookCorpus)
  • A dataset used in a specific commercial AI system (e.g. Stable Diffusion, GPT-4)
  • A civic AI dataset (e.g. one used in predictive policing, benefits screening, or hiring)

Instructions

  • Research the dataset's provenance: where was the data collected from? Who collected it? Were the sources consented?
  • Research the labor: who annotated, cleaned, or labeled it? Where? Under what pay and conditions?
  • Research the representation: what languages, communities, and viewpoints are over- or under-represented? What was excluded?
  • Create a supply chain diagram showing the journey from raw data to deployed model. At least 5 nodes.
  • Write a 400–500 word analysis drawing on at least two readings from the course.

Deliverable

Supply chain diagram + written analysis. Submit as PDF.

Grading

Accuracy and depth of provenance research35%
Supply chain diagram clarity25%
Written analysis with course readings40%
Assignment 3
"Probe the Machine"
due: week 5

Your Goal

Run a structured experiment with an LLM. This course is about AI — so you should interact with it directly, critically, and carefully. You are not testing whether the AI is "good" or "bad." You are using it as a site of investigation: a system whose infrastructure, training data, and governance decisions become legible through its outputs.

Instructions

  • Choose one of the following experiment types:
    • Language test: Ask the same substantive question in two or more languages. Compare the responses in depth — not just content, but tone, confidence, and what each version assumes about its audience.
    • Bias probe: Ask an LLM about a topic where you suspect it has a particular default perspective (geopolitical, cultural, ideological). Push back. Examine what it does when challenged.
    • Labor probe: Ask an LLM about its own training data, the people who labeled it, and the conditions under which it was built. Examine how it responds to questions about its own infrastructure.
  • Document your experiment fully: save all prompts and responses verbatim.
  • Write a 500–600 word analysis: what does the experiment reveal about the system's underlying infrastructure — its training data, its governance choices, its assumptions about users?
  • Cite at least two course readings in your analysis.

Deliverable

Full experiment log (screenshots or copied text) + written analysis. Submit as PDF.

Grading

Rigor and specificity of experiment design25%
Quality of documentation (full prompts and outputs)25%
Depth of written analysis35%
Integration of course readings15%
Assignment 4
"Read the Policy"
due: week 7

Your Goal

AI governance happens in documents: terms of service, acceptable use policies, regulatory filings, audit protocols, executive orders. These documents are dense, often deliberately vague, and full of choices that appear technical but are political. Your job is to read one carefully.

Choose One

  • The terms of service or usage policy for a major AI product (OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic, etc.)
  • A piece of AI legislation or regulatory guidance (EU AI Act provisions, EEOC guidance on AI hiring, NYC Local Law 144)
  • An internal AI governance document from a public institution (e.g. a university AI policy, the UVA Archival AI Protocol)
  • A corporate AI ethics statement or responsible AI framework

Instructions

  • Read the document carefully and completely.
  • Annotate it: mark where power is concentrated, where accountability is absent, what is defined vs. left undefined, and who the document seems to protect.
  • Write a 500-word close reading that answers: what does this document do? Who made it, for whom, and what interests does it serve? What is conspicuously absent?
  • Cite at least two course readings.

Deliverable

Annotated document (as PDF with annotations) + written close reading.

Grading

Quality and specificity of annotation30%
Depth of written close reading40%
Integration of course readings20%
Identification of what is absent10%
Assignment 5
"Adversarial Reading"
due: week 13

Your Goal

Drawing on DiSalvo's concept of adversarial design — design that makes conflict visible and produces political encounters — produce an adversarial reading of an AI product, system, or claim. This is not a balanced review. It is a deliberate, evidence-based critique that exposes something the system's designers or promoters would prefer to keep invisible.

Instructions

  • Choose one AI product, system, or public claim about AI (e.g. an AI company's safety commitments, a government's AI strategy, a product launch, a research result).
  • Apply the four-layer framework: what does each layer reveal that the surface presentation conceals?
  • Research it using primary sources — company filings, leaked documents, investigative journalism, academic studies, worker testimony — not just the company's own framing.
  • Produce an adversarial reading in a format of your choosing:
    • A written critical analysis (700–900 words)
    • An annotated visual (an ad, a product page, a press release — marked up with your analysis)
    • A counter-document (a rewritten terms of service, a shadow impact assessment, a dissenting policy brief)
  • Include a 200-word methodological note explaining your sources, your approach, and what you couldn't verify.

Deliverable

Adversarial reading (in chosen format) + methodological note. Submit as PDF.

Grading

Quality and specificity of the critique30%
Evidence: use of primary sources30%
Application of four-layer framework25%
Methodological note15%

🛠️ Midterm

Midterm
"Infrastructure Analysis"
due: week 8 · presentations week 9

Your Goal

Produce a complete, original infrastructure analysis of one AI system, platform, or dataset. This is an expanded, more rigorous version of Assignment 1 — but now informed by seven weeks of reading, discussion, and your follow-up work in Assignments 2–4. You should be able to say something specific, supported, and non-obvious about your subject.

Instructions

  • Choose an AI system, platform, or dataset that interests you. It can be something you analyzed before, or something new.
  • Apply all four layers of the infrastructure framework rigorously, using primary sources (company disclosures, investigative reporting, regulatory filings, academic studies) as well as course readings.
  • Write a 1,000–1,200 word analysis structured around a clear argument — not just a description of the four layers, but a claim about what those layers reveal together. What is the system's infrastructure concealing? Who does it serve? What conditions make it possible?
  • Include an infrastructure diagram (updated from or building on your Assignment 1 map).
  • Cite at least four course readings.

Midterm Presentation (Week 9)

  • 8–10 minute presentation of your analysis.
  • Walk us through your infrastructure diagram. State your argument clearly. What did you find that surprised you?
  • Peer feedback will focus on: is the argument legible? Is the evidence specific? What is the most important thing missing?

Grading

Clarity and strength of central argument25%
Depth and specificity of four-layer analysis30%
Use of primary sources and course readings25%
Infrastructure diagram10%
Presentation10%

Final Project
"Critical AI Portfolio"
due: week 17

Your Goal

The final project is a Critical AI Portfolio: a collected body of original work that demonstrates your ability to analyze AI as infrastructure across multiple registers — technical, political, cultural, and social. It should be something you could show as evidence of what you know how to do.

Portfolio Components

1. Revised Infrastructure Analysis (from Midterm)
Substantially revised in response to feedback. Should demonstrate deepened research, a sharper argument, and more specific evidence than the midterm version.

2. Adversarial Reading (Assignment 5, revised)
Revised and expanded based on peer and instructor feedback.

3. One New Piece
Produce one additional original work in a format of your choosing:

  • A close reading of a policy document not covered in class (600–800 words)
  • An extended LLM experiment with analysis (building on Assignment 3)
  • A data supply chain analysis of a system not covered in previous assignments
  • A comparative analysis: two AI systems, the same four-layer framework, one argument about what the comparison reveals

4. Reflective Introduction (400–500 words)
An essay introducing the portfolio: what questions drove your work this semester? What did you learn to see that you couldn't see before? What do you still not know how to analyze?

Final Presentation (Week 17)

  • 10–12 minutes. Walk us through your portfolio — not everything, but the thread that connects it.
  • State one thing you understand now that you didn't at the start of the semester. Be specific.
